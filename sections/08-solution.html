07 Take action

You can
take action.

Food access is not one problem, so there is no single fix. Across this story, three things stood out. Each one points to something you can do.

Good visualization starts with a specific question. Who is missing from the average? What changes when distance is measured by transit instead of miles? Which places become visible when you look at race, age, or income?

View the project on GitHub ↗
  1. 01

    Distance is only part of access.

    A store can be close on the map and still out of reach without a car, a bus line, or time to spare. The physical access maps show that low access clusters in particular places.

    Take action: Look up your own state and county in the map above, then ask local planners how they measure access to groceries and whether transit is counted.

  2. 02

    The average hides who is left out.

    When the data is split by race and by income, some groups face low access far more often than the national picture suggests. A single average can look fine while whole communities are not.

    Take action: Ask for local food-access numbers broken down by group, not just totals, and support organizations serving the neighborhoods the averages miss.

  3. 03

    A nearby store doesn't help if the budget can't stretch.

    Food benefits average about $1.41 per person per day. Food banks help, but the data counts their locations, not how much food they can give out. Access means being able to afford food, too.

    Take action: Tell your representatives you support food assistance, help eligible neighbors enroll, and give time or money to a local food bank.
